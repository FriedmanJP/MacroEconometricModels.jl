X-13ARIMA-SEATS

MacroEconometricModels.jl provides a pure-Julia implementation of the US Census Bureau's X-13ARIMA-SEATS seasonal adjustment program. The implementation ports automatic ARIMA model identification and two decomposition methods with zero external dependencies.

  • X-11: Iterative moving average decomposition (Shiskin, Young & Musgrave 1967) using Henderson trend filters and seasonal moving averages with extreme value replacement
  • SEATS: Signal Extraction in ARIMA Time Series (Gómez & Maravall 1996) via Wiener-Kolmogorov optimal filtering in the frequency domain
  • Automatic model identification: TRAMO-style search over seasonal ARIMA specifications with AICC-based selection
  • Automatic transformation choice: log versus level, decided by AICC with a Jacobian correction

Both decompositions are computed on every call; method selects which one the result reports. All results inherit from AbstractFilterResult and support trend(), cycle(), seasonal(), adjusted() accessors, report() for tabular output, refs() for bibliography, and plot_result() for interactive D3.js visualization.

Seasonal adjustment removes the within-year component; to strip the trend instead, see Time Series Filters. The seasonal ARIMA machinery underlying the model search is documented on ARIMA Models.

Every example uses the Box-Jenkins airline passengers series (Box & Jenkins 1976): 144 monthly counts of international airline passengers from 1949 to 1960, the canonical benchmark for seasonal adjustment.

X-11 Method

The X-11 method (Shiskin, Young & Musgrave 1967; Findley et al. 1998; Dagum & Bianconcini 2016) decomposes a time series into trend, seasonal, and irregular components through iterative phases of moving average smoothing. In the multiplicative model:

\[y_t = T_t \cdot S_t \cdot I_t\]

where:

  • $y_t$ is the observed time series
  • $T_t$ is the trend-cycle component
  • $S_t$ is the seasonal component
  • $I_t$ is the irregular component

The algorithm applies a $2 \times s$ centered moving average for initial trend estimation, computes seasonal-irregular (SI) ratios, extracts seasonal factors by averaging within each period across years, and refines the estimates using Henderson moving averages with extreme value detection. The multiplicative form is chosen automatically whenever the series is strictly positive; otherwise the components combine additively.

Technical Note

The implementation uses Census-standard seasonal moving averages with endpoint corrections from the original Fortran weight tables, and Henderson filters of length 13 for monthly and 5 for quarterly data. Seasonal factors are renormalized within each year — to mean 1 in the multiplicative model, mean 0 in the additive model — so the adjustment neither adds nor removes level.

SEATS Method

The SEATS method (Burman 1980; Maravall & Pierce 1987; Gómez & Maravall 1996) performs model-based seasonal adjustment using Wiener-Kolmogorov optimal signal extraction. Given an estimated ARIMA model for the observed series, SEATS allocates the model spectrum to trend, seasonal, and irregular components in the frequency domain.

\[S_y(\omega) = S_T(\omega) + S_S(\omega) + S_I(\omega)\]

where:

  • $S_y(\omega)$ is the model spectrum of the observed series
  • $S_T(\omega)$ is the trend spectrum, proportional to $1 / |\phi_T(e^{-i\omega})|^2$
  • $S_S(\omega)$ is the seasonal spectrum, proportional to $1 / |\phi_S(e^{-i\omega})|^2$
  • $S_I(\omega)$ is the irregular spectrum (flat, no AR denominator)
  • $\phi_T(B) = (1 - B)^d \cdot \phi(B)$ is the trend AR polynomial
  • $\phi_S(B) = (1 - B^s)^D \cdot \Phi(B^s)$ is the seasonal AR polynomial

The Wiener-Kolmogorov filter for each component is $H_c(\omega) = S_c(\omega) / S_y(\omega)$. These frequency-domain filters are converted to symmetric time-domain weights via inverse cosine transform and applied to the data with reflection boundary extension.

Technical Note

The spectrum is evaluated on a 2048-point frequency grid. Time-domain filter weights extend $\max\!\big(s,\ \min(3s,\ T/2 - 1,\ 120)\big)$ lags on each side, enough to capture at least one full seasonal cycle. Unlike X-11, SEATS produces minimum mean squared error estimates of each unobserved component.

r_seats = x13_filter(airline; frequency=12, method=:seats)
report(r_seats)

  Seasonal std              45.1282

SEATS decomposes the series on whatever scale the transformation step selected. For the airline data that scale is the level, so the SEATS seasonal component is additive — its standard deviation of $45.13$ is measured in passengers, not as a ratio, and is not comparable to the $0.1287$ X-11 figure. Both methods start from the same estimated ARIMA model; they differ only in how the spectrum is allocated to components, and the two seasonally adjusted series correlate at $0.995$. Pass transform=:log to make the SEATS components multiplicative and directly comparable to X-11.

Automatic Model Identification

The automatic model identification follows the TRAMO approach (Gómez & Maravall 1996). The algorithm searches over ARIMA$(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_s$ specifications in two phases:

  1. Differencing selection: Fit airline-type models $(0,d,1)(0,D,1)_s$ for $d \in \{0,1,2\}$ and $D \in \{0,1\}$; select $(d^*, D^*)$ by minimum AICC
  2. Order selection: With $(d^*, D^*)$ fixed, search 22 candidate $(p,q,P,Q)$ tuples — a focused set covering the most common seasonal ARIMA specifications, not an exhaustive grid

Estimation uses Conditional Sum of Squares (CSS) with Levenberg-Marquardt optimization, subject to $p + q \le 3$ and $P + Q \le 2$. The best model is selected by minimum corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICC):

\[\text{AICC} = -2 \ell + 2k + \frac{2k(k+1)}{n_{\text{eff}} - k - 1}\]

where:

  • $\ell$ is the CSS log-likelihood
  • $k$ is the total number of estimated parameters
  • $n_{\text{eff}}$ is the effective number of observations after differencing and filtering

If no candidate converges, the search falls back to $(0,1,0)$, then $(0,0,0)(0,1,0)_s$, then white noise.

r = x13_filter(airline; frequency=12)
r.arima_order, round(r.sigma2, digits=2), round(r.aic, digits=2)
((2, 1, 0, 1, 1, 1), 146.08, 829.31)

The search selects $(2,1,0)(1,1,1)_{12}$ on the untransformed airline series, with innovation variance $146.08$ and AIC $829.31$. Both differencing orders are one, as the strong trend and the annual cycle require, but the short-run dynamics come out as an AR(2) rather than the MA(1) of the textbook airline model — a direct consequence of fitting levels rather than logs. Fit the same series in logs and the search returns $(0,1,1)(0,1,0)_{12}$, much closer to the Box-Jenkins specification.

Log Transformation

The program selects between log and level specifications by fitting the airline model $(0,1,1)(0,1,1)_s$ to both $y_t$ and $\log y_t$, then comparing AICC values with a Jacobian correction for the change of variables:

\[\text{AICC}_{\log}^* = \text{AICC}_{\log} + 2 \sum_{t=1}^T \log y_t\]

The log transformation is selected when $\text{AICC}_{\log}^* + 2 < \text{AICC}_{\text{level}}$. A series containing any non-positive value is never log-transformed regardless of the criterion. Pass transform=:log or transform=:none to override the automatic choice.

r_auto = x13_filter(airline; frequency=12, transform=:auto)
r_log  = x13_filter(airline; frequency=12, transform=:log)
r_none = x13_filter(airline; frequency=12, transform=:none)

(auto = r_auto.transform, log = r_log.transform, none = r_none.transform)
(auto = :none, log = :log, none = :none)
The automatic choice disagrees with Census X-13 on the airline series

For the airline passengers data the criterion returns $\text{AICC}_{\text{level}} = 926.62$ against $\text{AICC}_{\log}^* = 1153.77$ and selects :none, even though the seasonal amplitude visibly grows with the level and the Census program selects the log. Set transform=:log explicitly whenever the series is positive and its seasonal swing scales with its level.

# Multiplicative seasonal factors once the log transform is imposed
r_log = x13_filter(airline; frequency=12, transform=:log)

(order = r_log.arima_order,
 seasonal_std = round(std(seasonal(r_log)), digits=4),
 irregular_std = round(std(cycle(r_log)), digits=4))
(order = (0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0), seasonal_std = 0.1277, irregular_std = 0.0153)

Forcing the log transform changes the answer substantially. The identified model becomes $(0,1,1)(0,1,0)_{12}$, and because the SEATS components are exponentiated back to the original scale they are now multiplicative factors: the seasonal standard deviation of $0.1277$ lines up almost exactly with the $0.1287$ X-11 produces, and the irregular standard deviation falls from $5.31$ (passengers) to $0.0153$ (a ratio). Two methods that appeared to disagree by three orders of magnitude were only reporting on different scales.

Keyword Arguments

KeywordTypeDefaultDescription
frequencyInt12Seasonal period (4 for quarterly, 12 for monthly; no other value is accepted)
methodSymbol:seatsWhich decomposition to report (:seats or :x11); both are always computed
transformSymbol:autoPre-transformation (:auto, :log, or :none)
startTuple{Int,Int}(1,1)Start date as (year, period) for calendar regressors
modelSymbol:autoARIMA model selection (:auto is the only supported value)
trading_dayBoolfalseInclude 6 trading day contrast regressors
easterBoolfalseInclude an Easter effect regressor
easter_windowInt8Days before Easter spanned by the Easter window
outliersBooltrueDetect additive outliers, level shifts, and temporary changes
critical_valueFloat640.0Outlier t-statistic threshold (0 selects 3.0–4.0 by series length)
Regression and outlier keywords have no effect in this release

x13_filter always drives the automatic identification path, and that path fits the ARIMA model without the regressor matrix and without the outlier search. As a result trading_day, easter, easter_window, outliers, critical_value, and model are accepted but inert: n_outliers is always $0$ and calendar regressors never enter the fit. Adjust for trading-day and Easter effects outside the call, or subtract known outliers from the input, until these paths are wired through.

Complete Example

This example adjusts the airline passengers series with both methods and quantifies how much month-to-month variation the adjustment removes:

r_x11   = x13_filter(airline; frequency=12, method=:x11)
r_seats = x13_filter(airline; frequency=12, method=:seats)

sa_x11, sa_seats = adjusted(r_x11), adjusted(r_seats)

(correlation = round(cor(sa_x11, sa_seats), digits=4),
 raw_volatility = round(std(diff(airline)), digits=2),
 x11_volatility = round(std(diff(sa_x11)), digits=2),
 seats_volatility = round(std(diff(sa_seats)), digits=2))
(correlation = 0.9949, raw_volatility = 33.75, x11_volatility = 7.13, seats_volatility = 10.37)
# Share of first-difference volatility removed by each method
(x11_pct = round(100 * (1 - std(diff(sa_x11)) / std(diff(airline))), digits=1),
 seats_pct = round(100 * (1 - std(diff(sa_seats)) / std(diff(airline))), digits=1))
(x11_pct = 78.9, seats_pct = 69.3)

The two seasonally adjusted series correlate at $0.9949$ — they agree on the path of underlying air travel — but they are not interchangeable. Month-to-month volatility falls from $33.75$ passengers in the raw data to $7.13$ under X-11 and $10.37$ under SEATS, so X-11 removes $78.9$ percent of first-difference variation against $69.3$ percent for SEATS. The gap reflects the additive SEATS decomposition on an untransformed series whose seasonal amplitude grows with the level: a single additive seasonal profile cannot match a swing that triples in size across the sample, while the X-11 multiplicative factors scale with it automatically. Impose transform=:log and the gap largely closes.

Common Pitfalls

  1. Series too short. x13_filter requires at least 3 full seasonal cycles — 36 observations for monthly, 12 for quarterly — and throws otherwise. Even at the minimum, seasonal factors rest on three observations per calendar period and are correspondingly noisy.

  2. Wrong frequency. Only frequency=4 and frequency=12 are accepted. Passing frequency=12 for quarterly data produces a nonsensical decomposition rather than an error, because 12 is a valid argument; the frequency must match the actual periodicity of the data.

  3. Comparing seasonal components across scales. X-11 seasonal factors are multiplicative ratios averaging one within each year. SEATS components are computed on the working scale, so they are additive unless a log transform was applied — the airline series gives $0.1287$ and $45.13$ for the same seasonal pattern. Compare seasonally adjusted series, not the seasonal components themselves, unless both are on the same scale.

  4. Trusting the automatic transformation. For a positive series whose seasonal amplitude grows with its level, the log specification is almost always the right one, but the automatic criterion does not always select it — it does not on the airline benchmark. Inspect r.transform and override with transform=:log when the plot says otherwise.

  5. Non-positive values with the log transform. The log transformation requires strictly positive values. If the series contains zeros or negatives, transform the data yourself before calling x13_filter; automatic selection silently falls back to :none in that case.

  6. Interpreting the cycle() accessor. For X13FilterResult, cycle() returns the irregular component, not a business cycle in the HP filter sense. This keeps the AbstractFilterResult interface uniform. Use seasonal() and adjusted() for the seasonal-adjustment-specific components, and see Time Series Filters for trend-cycle decomposition.

  7. Expecting calendar or outlier adjustment. The trading_day, easter, outliers, and critical_value keywords are currently inert (see the warning under Keyword Arguments). A result with n_outliers == 0 does not mean the series is outlier-free.
